Not the full lecture — just the definitions and rules you're actually tested on.

Core definitions

  • Pathology — study (logos) of suffering (pathos); structural & functional changes underlying disease.
  • Pathophysiology — the physiology of altered health; explains signs/symptoms, not treatment.
  • Disease — any condition impairing normal function of an organism.

Pathologist roles

  • Surgical pathologist — examines specimens from surgery (frozen/paraffin sections).
  • Clinical pathologist — lab-based: hematology, bacteriology, chemistry, serology.

Two branches of pathology

  • General pathology — basic cell/tissue reactions common to all disease.
  • Systemic pathology — specific organ responses to defined stimuli.

6 aspects of the disease process

  • 1. Etiology — the cause
  • 2. Pathogenesis — how it evolves
  • 3. Morphologic changes — structure
  • 4. Clinical manifestations — signs/symptoms
  • 5. Diagnosis — identification
  • 6. Clinical course — progression over time

Etiologic agents

  • Biologic — bacteria, viruses
  • Physical — trauma, burns, radiation
  • Chemical — poisons, alcohol
  • Nutritional — excess or deficit
  • Most disease is multifactorial → risk factors

Congenital vs. acquired

  • Congenital — present at birth (genetic, environmental, or both); may show later.
  • Acquired — after birth: injury, infection, poor nutrition, hypoxia, immune response, neoplasia.

Signs vs. symptoms

  • Symptom — subjective (pain, dizziness, dyspnea).
  • Sign — objective, observer-noted (fever, swelling, pupil change).
  • Either reflects the primary disorder or the body's compensation.

Syndrome · complication · sequela

  • Syndrome — a characteristic cluster of signs/symptoms.
  • Complication — adverse extension of disease or treatment.
  • Sequela — a lesion/impairment left behind by disease.

Diagnosis — two meanings

  • (1) Recognizing disease by its outward signs/symptoms.
  • (2) Analyzing the underlying physiologic/biochemical cause.
  • Process: history → physical exam → labs/imaging.

Normal values

  • Set statistically from a reference population.
  • Defined as mean ± 2 SD — the 95% distribution.
  • Differential diagnosis = the list of "rule-outs."

4 diagnostic modalities

  • Clinical — from history/exam (e.g. "acute meningitis").
  • Morphologic — from the lesion itself.
  • Etiologic — names the cause (e.g. "bacterial").
  • Disease/Definitive — names the disease itself.

Clinical course

  • Preclinical — too early to diagnose.
  • Acute — sudden, usually severe onset.
  • Subacute — moderate duration, in between.
  • Chronic — persists long-term, may disable.
  • Carrier — harbors the organism, no symptoms.

Epidemiology

  • Study of disease patterns/causes/effects in populations.
  • Endemic — maintained in a population, no external input.
  • Epidemic — new cases clearly exceed the expected baseline.
  • Pandemic — epidemic across continents (e.g. COVID-19).

High Yield

The facts, comparisons, and worked examples most likely to show up on an exam — distilled to one screen each.

Numbers★

The "normal" reference range

Statistically defined as mean ± 2 SD — the 95% distribution of results from a reference population, not an absolute cutoff.

Sequence★★

The 6 aspects of the disease process

Etiology → Pathogenesis → Morphologic changes → Clinical manifestations → Diagnosis → Clinical course. A favorite "put these in order" question.

Worked example★★★

4 diagnostic modalities — meningitis case

Clinical (acute meningitis) → Morphologic (inflamed pia/arachnoid) → Etiologic (bacterial) → Disease/definitive (Neisseria meningitis). Classic exam vignette.

Classic pair★★★

Sign vs. symptom

Symptom = subjective, patient-reported (pain, dizziness). Sign = objective, observer-noted (fever, swelling). The single most commonly confused pair.

Classic pair★★

Congenital vs. acquired

Congenital = present at birth (genetic and/or environmental), may show later. Acquired = arises after birth (injury, infection, nutrition, hypoxia, immune, neoplasia).

Classic pair★★★

Etiology vs. pathogenesis

Etiology = what starts it. Pathogenesis = how it evolves. Atherosclerosis is the pathogenesis of coronary heart disease — its true etiology is still uncertain.

Classic pair★★

Complication vs. sequela

Complication = adverse extension of disease or its treatment. Sequela = a lasting lesion/impairment left behind by the disease itself.

Scale★★★

Endemic → epidemic → pandemic

Endemic = steady baseline, no outside input. Epidemic = new cases clearly exceed that baseline. Pandemic = epidemic across continents (COVID-19, H1N1).

Sequence★★

Clinical course stages

Preclinical (too early to diagnose) → Acute (sudden, severe) → Subacute (moderate, patient looks well) → Chronic (long-term, may progress/disable).

Definition★★

Carrier

Harbors the disease organism with no symptoms, yet can still act as a distributor of infection to others.

Sequence★★★

Iceberg concept — bottom to top

Exposure without infection → infection without illness → mild illness → severe disease → death. Most of a population's disease sits in the hidden bottom layers.

Classification★★

Intrinsic vs. extrinsic vs. unknown

Intrinsic = from within (inherited, congenital, metabolic, psychogenic). Extrinsic = from outside (physical, chemical, iatrogenic, infectious). Unknown = idiopathic.

Classic pair★

General vs. systemic pathology

General = basic reactions shared by all disease. Systemic = specific responses of individual organs to defined stimuli.

Classic pair★

Surgical vs. clinical pathologist

Surgical = studies tissue from surgery, often intra-operatively. Clinical = lab-based — hematology, bacteriology, chemistry, serology.

Definition★★

Diagnosis — two definitions

(1) Recognizing disease by its outward signs/symptoms. (2) Analyzing its underlying physiological/biochemical cause. Examiners like to test both.

Definition★

Differential diagnosis

The list of possible diagnoses ("rule-outs") that could explain a case's history, signs, or lesions — not the final answer itself.

Definition + example★

Syndrome

A compilation of characteristic signs and symptoms — e.g. chronic fatigue syndrome — without necessarily naming one single causative disease.

Principle★★

Most disease is multifactorial

Cancer, heart disease, and diabetes rarely have one single cause — they arise from several combined risk factors instead.

Principle★★

Etiologic agents are mostly nonspecific

One agent can damage several organs, and several different agents can damage the same organ — which is exactly why differential diagnosis exists.
