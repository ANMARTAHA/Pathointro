01 · Core Idea

Pathophysiology is the physiology of altered health: the study of the biologic and physical manifestations of disease as they correlate with the underlying abnormalities and physiologic disturbances. It explains the processes in the body that result in the signs and symptoms of disease, and does not deal directly with treatment.

The lecture follows the disease process (etiology → pathogenesis → morphologic changes → clinical manifestations → diagnosis → clinical course) and ends with epidemiology.

02 · Learning Points
  • Define pathophysiology, pathology and pathologist; distinguish surgical vs clinical pathologist.
  • Divide pathology into general and systemic; place them in the Tree of Medicine.
  • Know the 6 aspects of the disease process and the difference between etiology and pathogenesis.
  • Classify causes: biologic, physical, chemical, nutritional; congenital vs acquired; intrinsic vs extrinsic vs unknown.
  • Differentiate symptom, sign, syndrome, complication, sequelae.
  • Understand diagnosis: its two meanings, the diagnostic process, normal values, differential diagnosis, and the 4 types of diagnosis.
  • Describe the clinical course (preclinical, acute, subacute, chronic, carrier) and the iceberg concept.
  • Define epidemiology, endemic, epidemic, pandemic.
03 · Key Definitions

Pathology & people

  • Pathology — Greek: study (logos) of suffering (pathos). A bridging discipline of basic science and clinical practice, devoted to structural and functional changes in cells, tissues and organs that underlie disease. Uses molecular, microbiologic, immunologic and morphologic techniques to explain the whys and wherefores of signs/symptoms, giving a sound foundation for rational clinical care and therapy.
  • Pathophysiology — study of biologic and physical manifestations of disease correlated with underlying abnormalities; not directly treatment.
  • Pathologist — physician specialist in pathology: practices, evaluates and supervises diagnostic tests using material removed from living or dead patients; laboratory consultant to clinicians; also conducts experiments/investigations to determine causes or nature of disease changes.
  • General pathology — basic reactions of cells and tissues to abnormal stimuli underlying all diseases.
  • Systemic pathology — specific responses of specialized organs and tissues to more or less well-defined stimuli.

Disease process terms

  • Disease — any condition that impairs the normal functioning of an organism or body.
  • Etiology — causes of disease (etiologic factors); what sets the disease process in motion.
  • Pathogenesis — sequence of events in the response of cells/tissues to the etiologic agent, from initial stimulus to ultimate expression of disease; how the process evolves.
  • Risk factors — the multiple factors that predispose to a particular disease.
  • Morphology — fundamental structure or form of cells or tissues.
  • Histology — study of cells and extracellular matrix of body tissues.
  • Histologic sections — thin, translucent sections of tissues/organs examined by microscope.
  • Lesion — pathologic or traumatic discontinuity of a body organ or tissue.

Clinical & population terms

  • Symptom — subjective complaint noted by the person with the disorder.
  • Sign — manifestation noted by an observer.
  • Syndrome — compilation of signs and symptoms characteristic of a specific disease state.
  • Complications — possible adverse extensions of a disease or outcomes from treatment.
  • Sequelae — lesions or impairments that follow or are caused by a disease.
  • Diagnosis — (1) recognition of a disease by its outward signs and symptoms; (2) analysis of the underlying physiological/biochemical cause(s).
  • Differential diagnosis ("rule-outs") — list of diagnoses that could account for the history, clinical signs or lesions in a case.
  • Carrier — harbors disease organisms without manifest symptoms, thus distributing infection.
  • Epidemiology — study of patterns, causes and effects of health and disease conditions in defined populations.
04 · Classification / Types

Pathologist

  • Surgical pathologist — studies disease by analysing tissue specimens obtained during surgery; often examines specimens during surgery to decide how the operation should be modified or completed.
  • Clinical pathologist — laboratory study of disease using techniques suited to the specimen. Branches: hematology, bacteriology, chemistry, serology.

Pathology

  • General pathology studies: inflammation; cell injury, adaptation and death; tissue repair; hemodynamic disorders; diseases of immunity; neoplasia; genetic and pediatric diseases; environmental diseases; infectious diseases.
  • Systemic pathology — organ/tissue-specific responses.

Etiologic agents

  • Biologic — bacteria, viruses
  • Physical forces — trauma, burns, radiation
  • Chemical — poisons, alcohol
  • Nutritional — excesses or deficits
  • Molecular level — distinguish abnormal molecules from molecules that cause disease

By time of onset

  • Congenital — present at birth, may not be evident until later. Causes: genetic, environmental (viral infection in mother, maternal drug use, irradiation, intrauterine crowding), or both.
  • Acquired — events after birth: injury, infectious agents, inadequate nutrition, lack of oxygen, inappropriate immune responses, neoplasia. Many diseases = genetic predisposition + environmental trigger.

Causes of disease (figure)

  • Intrinsic — inherited, congenital, metabolic, degenerative, neoplastic, immunologic, nutritional deficiency, psychogenic.
  • Extrinsic, inanimate (physical-agent induced) — force, temperature, humidity, radiation, electricity, chemicals; plus iatrogenic.
  • Extrinsic, animate (infectious) — pathogenic organisms: viruses, bacteria, fungi, protozoa; pathogenic animals: insects, worms.
  • Unknown — idiopathic.

Tree of Medicine

  • Roots — Anatomy, Embryology, Histology, Chemistry, Pharmacology, Physiology, Physics, Cell Biology, Immunology, Microbiology, Genetics.
  • Trunk — General Pathology.
  • Branch junction — Systemic Pathology.
  • Canopy — Internal Medicine, Neurology, Psychiatry, Dermatology, Ophthalmology, Anesthesiology, Surgery, Pediatrics, Dentistry, Radiology, Gynecology, Obstetrics.

Signs & symptoms

  • Either related to the primary disorder, or the body's attempt to compensate for altered function caused by the pathologic condition.

Types of diagnosis

  • Clinical — case history, clinical signs, physical examination.
  • Morphologic (lesion) — predominant lesion(s); gross or histologic; describes severity, duration, distribution, location, nature (degenerative, inflammatory, neoplastic).
  • Etiologic — definitive; names the specific or general cause.
  • Disease (definitive) — names the disease.

Clinical course & epidemiology

  • Preclinical · Acute · Subacute · Chronic (+ carrier).
  • Endemic · Epidemic · Pandemic.
  • Iceberg levels: exposure without infection; infection without clinical illness; mild illness; severe disease; death.
05 · Important Concepts
  • Nonspecific & multifactorial: most disease-causing agents are nonspecific — many agents can cause disease of one organ, and one agent/traumatic event can affect several organs/systems. Most diseases lack a single cause; the majority are multifactorial in origin, especially cancer, heart disease, diabetes.
  • Morphologic changes cover both gross anatomic and microscopic changes characteristic of a disease. Their nature and distribution in organs/tissues influence normal function and determine clinical features (symptoms and signs), course and prognosis.
  • Histologic sections are the most common method for studying tissue and play an important role in diagnosing many types of cancer. Lesion size/characteristics can be obtained by radiographs, ultrasonography and other imaging; lesions may be sampled by biopsy for histologic study.
  • Ways disease manifests: some conditions produce evident manifestations (e.g., fever); others are silent at onset and found during examination for other purposes or when far advanced.
  • Compensation: many pathologic states cannot be observed directly (one cannot see a sick heart or failing kidney); what is seen is the body's attempt to compensate.
  • Normal vs abnormal test results: normal values are set statistically from a selected sample; they represent the 95% distribution (mean ± 2 SD) of the reference population.
  • Epidemiology is the cornerstone of public health; informs policy and evidence-based medicine by identifying risk factors and targets for preventive medicine; studies risk factors of multifactorial diseases through patterns such as age, sex, nutrition, race, habits, lifestyle.
06 · Important Differences
  • Etiology vs Pathogenesis — etiology = what sets the disease in motion; pathogenesis = how it evolves. Often used interchangeably but meanings differ.
  • Symptom vs Sign — subjective (noted by patient) vs observed by someone else.
  • Congenital vs Acquired — present at birth vs caused by events after birth.
  • General vs Systemic pathology — basic reactions common to all disease vs specific organ/tissue responses.
  • Surgical vs Clinical pathologist — tissue specimens from surgery vs laboratory study of specimens.
  • Diagnosis (1) vs (2) — recognition by outward signs/symptoms vs analysis of underlying physiological/biochemical cause.
  • Syndrome vs Complication vs Sequelae — cluster of signs/symptoms vs adverse extension/outcome of treatment vs lesion/impairment following disease.
  • Acute vs Subacute vs Chronic — sudden/severe vs moderate duration or severity vs long-persisting, often less severe symptoms.
  • Endemic vs Epidemic vs Pandemic — maintained without external input vs cases substantially exceed expected vs epidemic of infectious disease across a large region/worldwide.
  • Abnormal molecules vs molecules that cause disease — must be distinguished at the molecular level.
07 · Processes / Stages

Disease process (6 aspects)

  • 1. Etiology
  • 2. Pathogenesis
  • 3. Morphologic changes
  • 4. Clinical manifestations (functional derangements)
  • 5. Diagnosis
  • 6. Clinical course

Diagnostic process

  • 1. Careful history — person's account of symptoms, progression and contributing factors.
  • 2. Physical examination — signs of altered body structure/function.
  • 3. Laboratory tests, radiologic studies, CT scans etc. — often used to confirm the diagnosis.

Clinical course

  • Preclinical — no specific diagnosis possible; adequate signs/symptoms not yet developed.
  • Acute — relatively sudden, usually severe symptoms. Outcome: recovery to prior health, passage into a chronic phase, or death.
  • Subacute — between acute and chronic; moderate duration or severity.
  • Chronic — persists long; may be progressive, cause complete/partial disability, or lead to death.

Other sequences

  • Pathogenesis: initial stimulus → ultimate expression of disease (e.g., fatty streak → occlusive vessel lesion).
  • Surgical specimen: appearance noted → slices by paraffin or frozen section → microscopic exam by pathologist.

09 · Examples

Concept examples

  • Etiology vs pathogenesis: atherosclerosis is often cited as the cause of coronary heart disease, but the progression from fatty streak to occlusive vessel lesion is the pathogenesis; true etiology of atherosclerosis remains largely uncertain.
  • Multifactorial: cancer, heart disease, diabetes.
  • Symptoms: pain, difficulty breathing, dizziness. Objective signs: elevated temperature, swollen extremity, change in pupil size. Evident manifestation: fever.
  • Compensation: tachycardia with blood loss; increased respiratory rate in pneumonia.
  • Syndrome: chronic fatigue syndrome.
  • Chronic disease: diabetes mellitus, emphysema, arthritis.
  • Pandemic: H1N1 influenza virus, COVID-19.

Diagnosis examples

  • Morphologic: "severe, acute, locally-extensive, fibrinous bronchopneumonia".
  • Etiologic: parvoviral enteritis, parasitic hepatitis.
  • Case: 10-year-old child, severe neck rigidity and vomiting for 2 days; died before complete work-up; necropsy performed.

10 · Clinical / Practical Importance
  • Pathology provides a sound foundation for rational clinical care and therapy; the pathologist acts as laboratory consultant to clinicians.
  • Surgical pathologists guide the operation itself by examining specimens intra-operatively.
  • Histologic sections are important in cancer diagnosis; imaging and biopsy characterize lesions.
  • Diagnosis needs history + examination, confirmed by lab, radiology and CT; interpretation depends on normal ranges (mean ± 2 SD); differential diagnosis lists the rule-outs.
  • Silent diseases may be found incidentally or late; carriers spread infection without symptoms.
  • Epidemiology identifies risk factors and targets for preventive medicine, and patterns (age, sex, nutrition, race, habits, lifestyle) that help doctors diagnose.
